# enunciated

The useful point is not that English speakers fail to pronounce unstressed syllables. The useful point is that many English varieties use a contrast between strong and weak syllables: stressed syllables tend to be longer, louder, clearer in vowel quality, and more likely to carry pitch movement, while unstressed syllables are often shorter and more centralized.

That is why spelling-based "full enunciation" can sound unnatural in ordinary English. In words such as `photograph`, `photography`, `about`, `support`, and `banana`, the vowel letters do not all keep the same quality. Unstressed vowels commonly reduce toward schwa /ə/, /ɪ/, or another weak vowel depending on the accent and word. This is not laziness. It is part of the phonological organization of English.

The traditional label "stress-timed language" should be used carefully. Dauer (1983) showed that English is not stress-timed in the literal sense that stresses recur at equal clock intervals. The better evidence-based claim is that English rhythm is stress-based relative to many languages because it combines complex syllable structure, vowel reduction, and strong effects of stress on phonetic realization. Some English varieties reduce vowels more than others, so the advice cannot be applied mechanically to every accent.

The learner's target should therefore be contrast, not maximum force. A clear English syllable is not always a fully spelled-out syllable. Clarity often comes from making stressed syllables perceptually available while allowing predictable unstressed material to be weak. Cutler and Norris (1988) found that listeners use strong syllables in English speech segmentation, and Cutler and Carter (1987) found that English vocabulary and spontaneous conversation strongly favor lexical words beginning with strong syllables. Those findings help explain why flattening every syllable into equal prominence can make speech harder, not easier, to parse.

This also matters for accent bias. People often mistake familiar reductions for "clear speech" and unfamiliar reductions for "slurring". That judgment is social as well as phonetic. Niedzielski (1999) showed that a social label could change how listeners categorized the same vowel tokens, and Rubin (1992) found that nonlanguage cues such as perceived ethnicity affected judgments of nonnative-speaking teaching assistants. The conclusion is not that all pronunciations are equally easy for all listeners in all contexts; it is that "clear" is partly conditioned by listener expectation and familiarity.

## Practical correction

Do not try to pronounce every vowel letter in full. Learn which syllable is stressed, keep the stressed syllable intelligible, and let common unstressed syllables reduce in the way expected by the target variety.
