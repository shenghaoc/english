# prestige change

The claim that prestige accents are naturally conservative is not supported by the historical record. Prestige varieties are social targets, not frozen systems. Once an innovation becomes socially acceptable in high-status networks, it can spread quickly and later be reinterpreted as normal or neutral.

Classic variationist work distinguishes stable variation from active change and shows that socially evaluated changes often move through higher-status groups in characteristic ways. Labov (1990) describes "change from above," where innovations that enter community awareness are often led by socially mobile and higher-status speakers, especially women. This is not evidence that prestige groups are always first in every change, but it does show that prestige and innovation are compatible rather than opposed.

Evidence from standard British pronunciation is especially clear. Harrington, Palethorpe, and Watson (2000) found measurable vowel movement in Queen Elizabeth II's speech across decades, toward mainstream Standard Southern British patterns. Harrington (2006) found parallel change in happY-tensing over time. Fabricius (2002) showed that /t/-glottalling had changing social status in modern RP, with environments once treated as stigmatized becoming less marked. These are direct counterexamples to the belief that prestige pronunciation is a fixed, conservative endpoint.

The key correction is therefore about tendency, not absolute law. Prestige accents are not always the fastest-changing in every variable, and nonprestige varieties can be highly innovative too. But the stereotype "prestige equals conservative and accentless" fails empirically. Prestige varieties also participate in change, can advance innovations quickly when socially rewarded, and often rewrite their own history by presenting new norms as timeless correctness.

This helps explain accent bias in ordinary judgments. People often take the prestige variety they learned in school or media as neutral and stable, then describe local forms as emotional, sloppy, or recent distortions. Linguistic evidence shows that both prestige and nonprestige varieties are accents with ongoing change.

## Practical correction

Treat prestige pronunciation guides as snapshots of moving targets. Use them for orientation, but avoid treating them as immutable standards or as evidence that one accent has no social history.
