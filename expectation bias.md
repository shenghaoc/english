# expectation bias

Perceived clarity is not determined by articulation alone. It is co-determined by what listeners expect to hear. In linguistics and speech perception research, intelligibility, comprehensibility, and accentedness are related but not identical. A speaker can sound accented without being hard to understand, and listener expectations can make the same acoustic signal easier or harder to process.

Munro and Derwing (1995) showed that stronger foreign-accent ratings did not map neatly onto lower intelligibility. In a second study, Munro and Derwing (1995) found processing-time costs for some accented speech, but also showed that comprehensibility is a distinct dimension. This matters because many everyday judgments collapse these categories into one moral label such as "clear" or "bad English."

Listener expectations also produce measurable bias. Babel and Russell (2015) showed that social information about the speaker can reduce intelligibility when expectations and signal do not align. Lev-Ari and Keysar (2010) found that accent reduced perceived credibility of true statements, which means social inference can contaminate factual judgment. Dragojevic and Giles (2016) showed that processing fluency is a key mediator of language attitudes: speech that feels easy is often evaluated more positively, even when that ease comes from familiarity rather than objective linguistic superiority.

The interlanguage intelligibility work by Bent and Bradlow (2003) further weakens the "one best accent" myth. They found cases where nonnative listeners understood nonnative speakers as well as, or better than, native listeners did, depending on talker-listener language pairing. That is strong evidence that intelligibility is interactional, not a one-dimensional property of a speaker.

This directly supports the broader argument in this repo: people tend to hear their own accent as normal, clear, and down-to-earth because it is familiar and easy for their own perceptual system. That is a bias pattern, not a neutral benchmark.

## Practical correction

When evaluating pronunciation, separate at least three questions: "How accented does it sound?", "How easy is it to understand?", and "How much am I influenced by familiarity with this accent?" Treating these as separate variables produces fairer, more linguistic, and less class-coded judgments.
