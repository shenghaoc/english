Hockchia stood at about 0.4% in the [1957 census](https://www.reddit.com/r/singapore/comments/oroq24/language_groups_in_singapore_in_1957/) and about 0.6% (17,070 people) in the [2020 census](https://data.gov.sg/datasets/d_7a942ef799c7a66a78835245bc581980/view) — relative growth, but still marginal. See the [NLB preview](https://www.nlb.gov.sg/main/book-detail?cmsuuid=7b306d60-16c1-458d-a9ef-6be4cb6b1821) for historical Foochow and Hockchia presence around the time of independence from the British. The repo owner has never heard the dialect spoken by strangers anywhere in Singapore except by people obviously dressed as labourers, who are almost certainly foreigners. If the major dialects are dying out, this one is already dead.

## Hockchia and Foochow

Hockchia (also spelled Hokchia; Fuqing, Futsing, Hokchiang) is an exonym for the dialect of Fuqing county — evident from the name of the Singapore Futsing Association. It differs from Foochow mostly in accent. Many Foochow people in Southeast Asia originate from outside the core Foochow-speaking counties of Min and Houguan and would have accommodated multiple accents; most of Fuqing and neighbouring Pingtan sound largely the same to each other.

## Hokkien and Teochew

Hokkien (literally the name of the entire province — a misleading exonym) is the label for the dialect of Quanzhou (including Xiamen) and Zhangzhou on the south Fujian coast, not for Fujian as a whole. It has significant mutual intelligibility with Teochew, spoken in the neighbouring part of Guangdong. Together they dwarf all other dialect groups except Cantonese. Even within Quanzhou and Zhangzhou there are many accents, before counting emigrants along neighbouring coasts and overseas.

Hokkien and Teochew have no mutual intelligibility with Foochow and Hockchia, despite the shared tendency to claim ancestry from Gushi County in modern-day Henan.

## Why Southern Min dominates

Foochow and Amoy were both treaty ports opened by the Treaty of Nanking, but Foochow was mostly dependent on the tea trade. When the British realized they could plant their own tea in their colonies, Foochow's significance was greatly diminished. Amoy, meanwhile, had ties to Taiwan and Southeast Asia. The Wikisource chapters on the two Fujian treaty ports ([Foochow](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Twentieth_Century_Impressions_of_Hongkong,_Shanghai,_and_other_Treaty_Ports_of_China/Foochow), [Amoy](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Twentieth_Century_Impressions_of_Hongkong,_Shanghai,_and_other_Treaty_Ports_of_China/Amoy)) already treated the split as fundamental: "Amoy is the port of foreign trade for South Fokien, a region which, though politically and administratively united with the northern half of the province of Fokien, is geographically and ethnologically distinct."

Varieties related to or derived from [Southern Min](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Min) (south Fujian) stretch as far north as Wenzhou in Zhejiang and as far south as Hainan island, formerly part of Guangdong. That dominance along China's southern coast is broken only in the regions surrounding Fujian's and Guangdong's respective provincial capitals. Taiwan's importance and influence further cements 闽南语 as the most well-known Fujian language, and the three Southern Min prefecture-level cities combined hold approximately half the population of Fujian.

Prestige within the group is its own question. Based on Lee Kuan Yew's [1984 Speak Mandarin Campaign speech](https://www.nas.gov.sg/archivesonline/data/pdfdoc/lky19840921a.pdf), Amoy was seen as the prestige variant in Singapore — he contrasted it with "pidgin Hokkien" — and the Capital 958 dialect broadcast specifies Amoy (厦语). (Wikipedia suggests the same, but the sentence has no citations.) Internally, though, Amoy is not always the prestige variant: for a long time in recent decades the Minnan region had the prefecture-level city with the highest total GDP (Quanzhou), and it still has the one with the highest average GDP (Xiamen). Xiamen/Amoy was formerly part of Quanzhou and borders Zhangzhou, making it a mixed variant — for certain words Taiwan's Taiwanese dictionary specifies separate Quanzhou, Zhangzhou, and mixed pronunciations (e.g. https://sutian.moe.edu.tw/zh-hant/su/11609/).

To quote the [Singapore Chinese Cultural Centre](https://culturepaedia.singaporeccc.org.sg/language-education/the-hokkien-dialect-in-singapore/): "Nevertheless, even though Hokkien is, strictly speaking, not equivalent to Minnan dialect, it has become customary in Singapore to use 'Hokkien' to refer to the Minnan dialect."

## Vocabulary, intonation, and geography

The popularity of the "Dear You" movie exposed the repo owner to some Teochew vocabulary and showed that the underlying core vocabulary of the Min dialects — the whole branch, not only Southern Min — is much more stable than expected, even into neighbouring Guangdong: 批 means letters in Hockchia too.

A general pattern: intonation tends to be more similar for Min varieties that are geographically closer together, while vocabulary and pronunciation tend to follow administrative boundaries. Two caveats: China's southeast is mostly mountains with small patches of plains along the coast, so neighbours are not as close as maps suggest; and the provincial capital and major port cities have significant exposure to influence from other provinces or countries.

For a Fuqing speaker, the intonation of neighbouring Putian (Henghua) is familiar but the vocabulary and pronunciation are mostly incomprehensible, while the prestige Fuzhou dialect has unfamiliar intonation and slightly different pronunciation with very similar vocabulary. South of Putian, the spoken form is impossible to understand without tracing the original Chinese characters of words to find similarities. But further does not necessarily mean more different: Foochow 诸娘囝 and Teochew 姿娘囝 are obvious cognates, while Hokkien 查某囡仔 indicates that although likely of common origin, the word is no longer analysed as a combination of the original characters.

Fujian province itself is perhaps the only place where most people are well aware that there is no mutual intelligibility between spoken languages that have diverged for more than a thousand years. Note that Chinese dialects (including Mandarin) tend to substitute characters based on pronunciation, because the original characters have long been forgotten and the current pronunciation can no longer be associated with them.

Intonation, pronunciation, and vocabulary drift even within the two historical Hokkien-speaking prefectures, so the terminology used by [Capital 958's dialect news](https://www.melisten.sg/podcast/playlist/CAPITAL-958-%E6%96%B9%E8%A8%80%E6%96%B0%E9%97%BB-545761) is the most conservative, least controversial, and most accurate the repo owner has seen in Singapore. Foochow is included — and hearing it there is what made the unfamiliarity of Foochow intonation apparent; see [tonal character](<tonal character.md>).

## Speak Mandarin Campaign and national identity

According to the NLB's article on the [Speak Mandarin Campaign](https://www.nlb.gov.sg/main/article-detail?cmsuuid=226f348a-2170-4719-9f45-ef57755e8cb8), the campaign addressed continued dialect use among Chinese Singaporeans, which complicated the bilingual education policy. In daily life, English had become the practical lingua franca across dialect groups while each group kept its own variety in private domains. Holding a native tongue plus one lingua franca is rational; a sub-lingua franca is not. Having a sub-lingua franca when there is already a lingua franca leads to confusion — it leads many people to believe that Mandarin was somehow forced upon them, even though it was an entirely voluntary decision by their own community leaders, and in many scenarios there is inevitably no room for a sub-lingua franca at all. Refer to [Lee Kuan Yew's 1979 speech](https://www.nas.gov.sg/archivesonline/lky100av2) ([transcript](https://www.nas.gov.sg/archivesonline/data/pdfdoc/lky19790907.pdf)) launching the campaign: the natural outcome without the dialect ban would have been Chinese people speaking English with one another — which has come to pass even with the campaign.

Mandarin as the common Chinese language taught in Singapore schools dates back to at least 1916 at [Tao Nan School](https://www.roots.gov.sg/places/places-landing/places/national-monuments/former-tao-nan-school-now-the-peranakan-museum), an intentional decision by leaders of the Hokkien community to switch away from Hokkien. Widespread adoption of Mandarin was the result of the Republic of China's New Culture Movement spreading to the diaspora in Southeast Asia: both the first Chinese high school and the first Chinese university in Southeast Asia taught in Mandarin, and Mandarin replaced Classical Chinese as the written form even in Hong Kong. It would have been extremely strange for Singapore to try to teach the true mother tongues instead of Mandarin and invent dialect education from scratch.

From the repo owner's perspective as a member of a minority dialect group that was present when the country was founded, promoting Mandarin was and is the right decision. The ambivalence lies elsewhere: the dialect broadcast ban also restricts the number of "Dear You" screenings in the original Teochew, which is hard to cheer for when one's own dialect is already effectively dead here.

The decline of Chinese dialects creates a situation where the majority-dialect-influenced English accent becomes a symbol of national identity. Resisting it as an immigrant can be read as refusal to assimilate — as though only the first three or five dialect groups count as local.

## Immigration and citizenship

When illustrating the difficulty for non-Chinese minorities to get Singapore citizenship, it is disingenuous to count years spent in Singapore as a minor. It was once possible to apply for citizenship as a Full-Time National Serviceman (NSF), but by the time the repo owner enlisted in 2016 the scheme was no longer available; in late 2017 students became eligible, but not NSFs. He applied after finishing full-time service — almost 15 years after arriving, longer than most people he met during the mandatory sharing session, except for e.g. some older people who intentionally held on to their original citizenship.

But countries with no jus soli or jus sanguinis generally value economic contributions most highly. Measured that way, the repo owner obtained citizenship after zero years of economic productivity, while others at the Citizenship Journey sharing session had typically been economically productive in Singapore for about three to five years. Inflating 0 to 15 misstates the case: regardless of race, being able to obtain citizenship before contributing to the economy should be considered exceptionally fast and convenient, not a long arduous struggle.

Meeting cousins who are second-generation immigrants was a reminder that there is a straightforward explanation for the repo owner's situation: a first-generation immigrant who was not a citizen of the country he grew up in until he could finally apply at the ripe old age of 21, after two years as a conscript.

## Relation to English pronunciation

Southern Min (south Fujian) — the Hokkien/Minnan exonyms refer to the Quanzhou–Zhangzhou coast — is the dominant substrate for Singapore English and Singlish phonology at population scale (Bao, 2015; Leimgruber, 2013). The repo owner's variety, Hockchia (Eastern Min, east Fujian), is a small minority substrate. That asymmetry matters for [thick accent](<../thick accent.md>): locally valorised "clear English" features often track Southern Min syllable structure, not the owner's Eastern Min habits. See [tonal character](<tonal character.md>) for how the three Fujian varieties differ in pitch organisation.
