Dialect groups among Chinese Singaporeans, in order of descending population size. Each row marks a significant step down in magnitude:

1. Hokkien, Teochew, Cantonese
2. Hainanese, Hakka
3. **Foochow**
4. Henghua, Shanghainese, **Hockchia**
5. Mandarin

Hockchia stood at about 0.4% of the Chinese population in the [1957 census](https://www.reddit.com/r/singapore/comments/oroq24/language_groups_in_singapore_in_1957/) and about 0.6% (17,070 people) in the [2020 census](https://data.gov.sg/datasets/d_7a942ef799c7a66a78835245bc581980/view) — relative growth, but marginal in both years. The [NLB's records](https://www.nlb.gov.sg/main/book-detail?cmsuuid=7b306d60-16c1-458d-a9ef-6be4cb6b1821) document the Foochow and Hockchia presence around the time of independence from the British. Today the repo owner never hears the dialect from strangers in Singapore, except from people dressed as labourers — almost certainly foreigners. If the major dialects are dying out, this one is already dead.

## Hockchia and Foochow

Hockchia (also spelled Hokchia; Fuqing, Futsing, Hokchiang) is an exonym for the dialect of Fuqing county, as the name of the Singapore Futsing Association shows. It differs from Foochow mostly in accent. Many Foochow people in Southeast Asia originate from outside the core Foochow-speaking counties of Min and Houguan and would have accommodated multiple accents; most of Fuqing and neighbouring Pingtan sound largely the same to each other.

## Hokkien and Teochew

Hokkien — literally the name of the entire province, and therefore a misleading exonym — is the label for the dialect of Quanzhou (including Xiamen) and Zhangzhou on the south Fujian coast, not for Fujian as a whole. As the [Singapore Chinese Cultural Centre](https://culturepaedia.singaporeccc.org.sg/language-education/the-hokkien-dialect-in-singapore/) puts it: "Nevertheless, even though Hokkien is, strictly speaking, not equivalent to Minnan dialect, it has become customary in Singapore to use 'Hokkien' to refer to the Minnan dialect."

Hokkien has significant mutual intelligibility with Teochew, spoken in the neighbouring part of Guangdong; together the two groups dwarf every other dialect group except Cantonese. Even within Quanzhou and Zhangzhou there are many accents, before counting emigrants along neighbouring coasts and overseas. Hokkien and Teochew have no mutual intelligibility with Foochow and Hockchia, despite the shared tendency of all four groups to claim ancestry from Gushi County in modern-day Henan.

## Why Southern Min dominates

The dominance of Southern Min (south Fujian) among emigrant communities has historical and economic roots.

Foochow and Amoy were both treaty ports opened by the Treaty of Nanking, but their fortunes diverged. Foochow depended mostly on the tea trade, and once the British planted tea in their own colonies its significance collapsed. Amoy, by contrast, kept lasting ties to Taiwan and Southeast Asia. Contemporary observers treated the divide as more than administrative: the Wikisource chapters on the two ports ([Foochow](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Twentieth_Century_Impressions_of_Hongkong,_Shanghai,_and_other_Treaty_Ports_of_China/Foochow), [Amoy](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Twentieth_Century_Impressions_of_Hongkong,_Shanghai,_and_other_Treaty_Ports_of_China/Amoy)) note that "Amoy is the port of foreign trade for South Fokien, a region which, though politically and administratively united with the northern half of the province of Fokien, is geographically and ethnologically distinct."

The group's reach extends far beyond its home coast. Varieties related to or derived from [Southern Min](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Min) stretch north to Wenzhou in Zhejiang and south to Hainan island, formerly part of Guangdong; their dominance along China's southern coast is broken only around Fujian's and Guangdong's provincial capitals. Taiwan's cultural and economic weight further cements 闽南语 as the most widely known Fujian language, and the three Southern Min prefecture-level cities together hold roughly half the population of Fujian.

Prestige within the group is a separate question. In Singapore, Amoy was treated as the prestige variant: Lee Kuan Yew's [1984 Speak Mandarin Campaign speech](https://www.nas.gov.sg/archivesonline/data/pdfdoc/lky19840921a.pdf) contrasts it with "pidgin Hokkien", and the Capital 958 dialect broadcast still specifies Amoy (厦语). Within Fujian the picture is less clear-cut: Quanzhou long had the prefecture-level city with the highest total GDP in the region, Xiamen still has the highest GDP per person, and Xiamen/Amoy — formerly part of Quanzhou, bordering Zhangzhou — is itself a mixed variant. For some words, Taiwan's Ministry of Education dictionary records separate Quanzhou, Zhangzhou, and mixed pronunciations ([example](https://sutian.moe.edu.tw/zh-hant/su/11609/)).

## Vocabulary, intonation, and geography

The Min branch as a whole is more internally stable than its mutual unintelligibility suggests. The popularity of the movie "Dear You" exposed the repo owner to Teochew vocabulary and revealed that the branch's core vocabulary persists even into neighbouring Guangdong: 批 means letters in Hockchia too.

A general pattern emerges from such comparisons: intonation tends to track geography, while vocabulary and pronunciation tend to follow administrative boundaries. Two caveats apply. China's southeast is mostly mountains with small patches of coastal plain, so neighbours are not as close as maps suggest; and provincial capitals and major ports absorb outside influence.

The pattern is audible from Fuqing. Neighbouring Putian (Henghua) has familiar intonation but mostly incomprehensible vocabulary and pronunciation, while prestige Fuzhou has very similar vocabulary and only slightly different pronunciation, yet unfamiliar intonation. South of Putian, the spoken forms are impossible to follow without tracing words back to their original characters. Distance is not everything, though: Foochow 诸娘囝 and Teochew 姿娘囝 are obvious cognates, while the Hokkien equivalent 查某囡仔, though likely of common origin, is no longer analysable as a combination of the original characters.

Fujian may be the only place where most people are fully aware that spoken languages which diverged more than a thousand years ago share no mutual intelligibility. Comparison is further complicated by the tendency of Chinese dialects, Mandarin included, to substitute characters based on pronunciation once the original characters are forgotten, so that a current pronunciation can no longer be associated with its etymological character.

Because intonation, pronunciation, and vocabulary drift even within the two historical Hokkien-speaking prefectures, the terminology used by [Capital 958's dialect news](https://www.melisten.sg/podcast/playlist/CAPITAL-958-%E6%96%B9%E8%A8%80%E6%96%B0%E9%97%BB-545761) is the most conservative, least controversial, and most accurate the repo owner has seen in Singapore. Foochow is on the roster — and hearing it there is what revealed how unfamiliar Foochow intonation actually is; see [tonal character](<tonal character.md>).

## Speak Mandarin Campaign and national identity

According to the NLB's article on the [Speak Mandarin Campaign](https://www.nlb.gov.sg/main/article-detail?cmsuuid=226f348a-2170-4719-9f45-ef57755e8cb8), the campaign addressed continued dialect use among Chinese Singaporeans, which complicated the bilingual education policy. By then English had already become the practical lingua franca across dialect groups, with each group keeping its own variety in private domains.

Mandarin's role was not invented by the campaign. As the common Chinese language of Singapore schools it dates back to at least 1916 at [Tao Nan School](https://www.roots.gov.sg/places/places-landing/places/national-monuments/former-tao-nan-school-now-the-peranakan-museum), where leaders of the Hokkien community deliberately switched away from Hokkien. The wider adoption of Mandarin followed the Republic of China's New Culture Movement as it spread to the Southeast Asian diaspora: the first Chinese high school and the first Chinese university in Southeast Asia both taught in Mandarin, and Mandarin replaced Classical Chinese as the written standard even in Hong Kong. It would have been extremely strange for Singapore to teach the true mother tongues instead and invent dialect education from scratch.

Holding a native tongue plus one lingua franca is rational; holding a sub-lingua franca as well is not. When a sub-lingua franca exists alongside the real one, confusion follows — including the widespread belief that Mandarin was forced on the community, when the switch was a voluntary decision by its own leaders. [Lee Kuan Yew's 1979 speech](https://www.nas.gov.sg/archivesonline/lky100av2) ([transcript](https://www.nas.gov.sg/archivesonline/data/pdfdoc/lky19790907.pdf)) launching the campaign predicted that without the dialect restrictions, Chinese Singaporeans would end up speaking English with one another. That outcome has come to pass even with the campaign.

From the repo owner's perspective as a member of a minority dialect group present at the country's founding, promoting Mandarin was and is the right decision. The ambivalence lies elsewhere: the dialect broadcast ban also limits the number of "Dear You" screenings in the original Teochew — hard to cheer for when one's own dialect is already effectively dead here.

The decline of Chinese dialects has one further consequence for this repo: the majority-dialect-influenced English accent becomes a symbol of national identity. Resisting it as an immigrant can be read as refusal to assimilate — as though only the first three or five dialect groups count as local.

## Immigration and citizenship

A note on how the immigrant experience behind this repo should be measured. When illustrating the difficulty for non-Chinese minorities to obtain Singapore citizenship, it is disingenuous to count years spent in Singapore as a minor. Citizenship was once open to Full-Time National Servicemen, but the scheme was gone by the time the repo owner enlisted in 2016, and the late-2017 expansion covered students but not NSFs. He applied after completing full-time service — almost fifteen years after arriving, longer than nearly everyone he met at the mandatory sharing session, apart from some older attendees who had intentionally kept their original citizenship.

Yet in countries with neither jus soli nor jus sanguinis, economic contribution is what counts most. By that measure the repo owner received citizenship after zero years of economic productivity, while others at the Citizenship Journey session had typically contributed for three to five years. Inflating zero to fifteen misstates the case: regardless of race, obtaining citizenship before contributing to the economy is exceptionally fast and convenient, not a long arduous struggle.

Meeting cousins who are second-generation immigrants put the situation plainly: the repo owner is a first-generation immigrant who was not a citizen of the country he grew up in until he could finally apply, at the ripe old age of 21, after two years as a conscript.

## Relation to English pronunciation

Southern Min — the Hokkien/Minnan exonyms refer to the Quanzhou–Zhangzhou coast — is the dominant substrate for Singapore English and Singlish phonology at population scale (Bao, 2015; Leimgruber, 2013). The repo owner's variety, Hockchia (Eastern Min, east Fujian), is a small minority substrate. That asymmetry matters for [thick accent](<../thick accent.md>): locally valorised "clear English" features often track Southern Min syllable structure, not the owner's Eastern Min habits. See [tonal character](<tonal character.md>) for how the three Fujian varieties differ in pitch organisation.
