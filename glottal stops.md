# glottal stops

Connected speech is not slurring. It is the normal result of planning words in phrases rather than as isolated dictionary items. In fluent speech, articulatory gestures overlap: the tongue, lips, velum, and larynx are already preparing for nearby sounds while the current sound is being produced. Browman and Goldstein's work on articulatory phonology is a standard account of why coarticulation, assimilation, allophonic variation, and fluent-speech alternations are expected parts of speech rather than defects.

The original point needs correction because connected speech is not mainly a byproduct of avoiding word-final glottal stops. English connected speech includes linking /r/, intrusive /r/ in some non-rhotic accents, assimilation as in `green park` sounding closer to `greem park`, elision in high-frequency phrases, vowel reduction, flapping in many North American varieties, and changes in release across word boundaries. Glottal stops are only one part of the story.

A glottal stop [ʔ] is a closure at the glottis. It can replace or reinforce /t/ in many English accents, especially in syllable-final position. It is not inherently "crass", "lazy", or unintelligible. Fabricius (2002) documents /t/-glottalling as an ongoing change in modern Received Pronunciation, with stigma decreasing in some environments. That matters because RP is a prestige accent: even prestige varieties innovate, and their innovations can later be reinterpreted as normal.

Still, glottal replacement can affect how English words connect. If a speaker replaces many final oral consonants with a glottal closure, especially where the target variety expects an audible consonant, release, or liaison into the next word, the result may block cues that listeners expect. For example, `pick it up`, `put it on`, and `not at all` rely on word-boundary timing and consonant-vowel transitions. A glottal stop is not wrong by itself, but overusing it as a universal final consonant can reduce contrast and make the speech sound unlike the intended target variety.

The key distinction is between systematic accent features and loss of contrast. Native English accents differ in when /t/ is aspirated, unreleased, tapped, glottalized, or fully released. Those patterns are rule-governed. Learners run into trouble when advice collapses all of this into moral labels such as "slurring" or "fake accent". Gaskell and Marslen-Wilson (1996) show that listeners can handle phonologically regular variation in context; what disrupts recognition is not variation itself, but variation that is unexpected or not licensed by the phonological context.

## Practical correction

Treat connected speech as phrase-level pronunciation, not as carelessness. Learn the target variety's patterns for linking, reduction, assimilation, and /t/ realization. Do not replace every final consonant with a glottal stop, but also do not assume that every glottal stop is substandard.

## The social life of glottal stops

The stigmatization of glottal stops in English is a clear example of how linguistic features acquire social meaning that has nothing to do with their acoustic or communicative properties. Historically, /t/-glottalling was associated with working-class London speech (Cockney) and was heavily stigmatized in prescriptive discourse. However, Fabricius (2002) documents its spread into Received Pronunciation, particularly among younger RP speakers, with decreasing stigma in word-medial pre-consonantal positions (as in *football*, *network*).

This change demonstrates that prestige varieties are not static or conservative. They adopt features from other varieties, and those features then become reinterpreted as acceptable or even prestigious. The same feature that was "lazy" or "common" in one generation becomes unremarkable in the next—not because the acoustic properties changed, but because the social associations changed.

Trudgill (1974) documented /t/-glottalling in Norwich English and found it was a stable sociolinguistic variable, with higher rates in working-class speech and in casual styles. Importantly, the feature was systematic and rule-governed, not random "slurring." Speakers used it more in predictable phonological environments and less in careful speech, showing that it was part of their phonological competence.

## Connected speech processes are universal

Every language has connected speech processes. French has liaison and enchaînement, Italian has *raddoppiamento sintattico*, Korean has complex consonant assimilation rules, and Mandarin has tone sandhi. These are not defects; they are part of how languages are organized at the phrase level rather than the word level.

English connected speech includes:

- **Linking /r/**: In non-rhotic accents, /r/ appears between a word ending in a non-high vowel and a following vowel (*far away* [fɑːrəweɪ])
- **Intrusive /r/**: Extension of linking /r/ to contexts where there is no historical /r/ (*idea of* [aɪdɪərəv])
- **Assimilation**: Place assimilation (*ten boys* → [tem bɔɪz]), voicing assimilation, manner assimilation
- **Elision**: Deletion of /t/ and /d/ in consonant clusters (*next day* → [neks deɪ])
- **Flapping**: In North American English, /t/ and /d/ become a tap [ɾ] between vowels (*butter*, *ladder*)
- **Coalescence**: Adjacent consonants merge (*did you* → [dɪdʒu])

Nolan (1992) provides a comprehensive overview of English connected speech processes, showing that they are phonologically conditioned and predictable. They are not evidence of carelessness but of fluent speech planning.

## The double standard in accent evaluation

When prestige variety speakers use connected speech processes, they are described as speaking "naturally" or "fluently." When non-prestige variety speakers use different (but equally systematic) processes, they are described as "mumbling" or "slurring." This double standard is well-documented in sociolinguistics.

Lippi-Green (2012) analyzes language subordination in the United States, showing how accent discrimination functions as a proxy for discrimination based on race, ethnicity, and class. The claim that certain accents are "unclear" or "hard to understand" often reflects the listener's unfamiliarity and social attitudes rather than objective acoustic properties.

Clopper and Bradlow (2008) found that listeners' ability to transcribe speech from different English dialects was strongly affected by their familiarity with those dialects. Crucially, there was no single "most intelligible" dialect—intelligibility was relative to the listener's experience. This undermines claims that any particular accent is objectively clearer.

## Glottal reinforcement vs. glottal replacement

It's important to distinguish between glottal reinforcement (where a glottal closure accompanies an oral closure) and glottal replacement (where the glottal closure substitutes for the oral closure). Many English varieties use glottal reinforcement of /p/, /t/, /k/ in certain positions without losing the oral gesture entirely. This is different from complete replacement.

Docherty and Foulkes (1999) studied glottal variants of /t/ in Tyneside English and found a range of realizations from full oral closure to full glottal replacement, with many intermediate forms. The choice among variants was conditioned by phonological position, speech style, and social factors. Importantly, listeners familiar with the variety had no difficulty processing any of these variants.

The practical implication is that learners should aim to match the target variety's pattern, not to avoid glottal stops entirely. If the target is a variety that uses glottal reinforcement or replacement in certain positions, using it appropriately is part of sounding natural in that variety.
